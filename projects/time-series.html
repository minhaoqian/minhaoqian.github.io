Time Series Analysis — US Services Trade Balance

Exploratory analysis

The Services series is analysed. It represents the monthly United States trade balance in services from January 1992 to December 2024. A time series plot of the Services series shows a clear upward trend over time, indicating that the series is unlikely to be stationary in mean. The variability appears broadly stable, although fluctuations are somewhat larger in the later part of the sample. There are no obvious outliers, and all observations are retained for the analysis.

The autocorrelation function (ACF) of the original series shows very slow decay, remaining strongly positive even at relatively high lags. This behaviour is characteristic of a nonstationary series. The partial autocorrelation function (PACF) shows a strong spike at lag 1 followed by much smaller values, suggesting substantial persistence in the data.

To address the apparent nonstationarity, first differences of the series were considered. The differenced series fluctuates around zero, indicating that the trend has been effectively removed. Its variability is also substantially smaller than that of the original series. These features are consistent with a stationary series after differencing, and therefore support the use of an integrated model with one order of differencing. The ACF of the differenced series shows a rapid drop after lag 1, with most autocorrelations close to zero apart from a few small spikes. The PACF of the differenced series shows only weak and irregular behaviour, with no clear sustained pattern. These features are consistent with a short-memory stationary process.

Monthly boxplots show some variation across the year, but the medians and spreads are broadly similar across months, indicating no strong seasonal pattern. The boxplots of the differenced series also indicate only weak seasonal effects. There is therefore only limited evidence of seasonality from the exploratory analysis. On this basis, an initial ARIMA(1,1,1) model is proposed. This is a natural starting point, as the original series appears nonstationary. The differenced series exhibits short-memory dependence that can be captured by a low-order ARMA model.

Model development

The initial ARIMA(1,1,1) model was fitted to capture the dependence structure of the differenced Services series. As the verification step in the Box–Jenkins procedure, the residuals from this initial model were examined. The main aim was to assess whether the model had removed the serial dependence. For a satisfactory model, the residuals should behave approximately as white noise, with no substantial autocorrelation remaining.

Inspection of the residual autocorrelation function shows that most autocorrelations lie within the approximate 95% confidence bounds. However, there is some weak evidence of remaining dependence at higher lags. This suggests that the ARIMA(1,1,1) model captures much of the short-run dependence, but does not fully capture the dependence structure. This conclusion is supported by the Ljung–Box test, which provides a formal test for residual autocorrelation. The test yields a p-value of 0.014, indicating residual autocorrelation. Therefore, the initial non-seasonal ARIMA(1,1,1) model is not adequate.

These results suggest that additional structure remains, particularly at seasonal lags, motivating the inclusion of a seasonal component. Although seasonality was not strong, the exploratory analysis indicated some weak annual structure, with possible dependence at lag 12. This suggests that the non-seasonal ARIMA model may be improved by including a seasonal term. A seasonal extension of the model was therefore fitted, namely ARIMA(1,1,1) × (1,0,0)_{12}. This adds a seasonal autoregressive component at lag 12, capturing possible annual dependence while keeping the model simple.

Diagnostics for the seasonal model indicate an improvement. The residual autocorrelations are smaller and show no clear pattern, suggesting that the remaining dependence has been largely removed. The residuals therefore appear consistent with white noise. The seasonal model is preferred as the final model because it provides a more plausible account of the weak seasonal structure suggested by the exploratory analysis, without introducing unnecessary complexity. In particular, it retains the non-seasonal ARIMA(1,1,1) structure suggested by the differenced series, while also allowing for a modest seasonal effect at the annual lag. The final model is ARIMA(1,1,1) × (1,0,0)_{12}. This model balances fit and parsimony, capturing nonstationarity through differencing, short-run dependence through the ARIMA(1,1,1) component, and weak annual structure through the seasonal AR term.

Final model and forecasts

The final model selected is ARIMA(1,1,1) × (1,0,0)_{12}.

Let \(Y_t\) denote the observed series and \(\varepsilon_t\) white noise with variance \(\sigma^2\). The model can be written as:

\[ (1 - \phi_1 B)(1 - \Phi_1 B^{12})(1 - B)Y_t = (1 + \theta_1 B)\varepsilon_t, \]

with estimated parameters \(\phi_1 = -0.7072\), \(\theta_1 = 0.6732\), and \(\Phi_1 = -0.0985\).

Substituting these estimates gives \[ (1 + 0.7072B)(1 + 0.0985B^{12})(1 - B)Y_t = (1 + 0.6732B)\varepsilon_t. \] The estimated innovation variance is \(\sigma^2 = 0.3294\), as obtained from the fitted model output.

Forecasts for the next 12 months (January 2025 to December 2025) were obtained using the fitted model. The point forecasts remain relatively stable, fluctuating around approximately 25.7, indicating that the model predicts a continuation of the recent level of the series rather than further strong growth or decline.

The associated 95% prediction intervals were constructed using the standard formula

\[ \hat{Y}_{T+h} \pm 1.96 \cdot \mathrm{SE}(\hat{Y}_{T+h}) \]

The prediction intervals widen steadily as the forecast horizon increases, reflecting increasing forecast uncertainty. For example, the standard error increases from approximately 0.57 for January 2025 to around 1.95 for December 2025, leading to substantially wider prediction intervals at longer horizons.

The numerical values of the forecasts and their corresponding 95% prediction intervals are provided in Part 5.

Caveats and limitations

Although the selected ARIMA(1,1,1) × (1,0,0)_{12} model provides a reasonable fit to the data, there are several limitations.

First, the model assumes that the underlying data-generating process is linear and characterised by constant parameters over time. In practice, macroeconomic series such as the United States services trade balance are influenced by structural changes, policy shifts, and external shocks. For example, events such as financial crises or changes in global trade conditions may alter the behaviour of the series in ways that are not captured by a fixed-parameter ARIMA model. This may reduce the reliability of forecasts, particularly over longer horizons.

Second, although a seasonal autoregressive term at lag 12 has been included, the model only captures a relatively simple form of seasonality. The exploratory analysis suggested that seasonal effects are weak, and it is therefore possible that the seasonal component is either insufficient to capture more complex seasonal patterns or unnecessary. Any remaining seasonal structure not accounted for by the model may lead to residual dependence and reduced forecast accuracy.

Third, the model is purely univariate and does not incorporate any external explanatory variables. In reality, the services trade balance is likely to depend on a range of economic factors, such as exchange rates, global demand, and domestic economic conditions. By ignoring these factors, the model may fail to capture important drivers of variation, limiting its predictive performance.

Finally, the prediction intervals are based on the assumption that the model is correctly specified and that the innovations are approximately normally distributed. If these assumptions are violated, the nominal 95% prediction intervals may not achieve correct coverage. In addition, the increasing width of the prediction intervals over time, as observed in Part 3, highlights the growing uncertainty associated with longer-term forecasts.

Overall, while the model provides a simple and interpretable framework for short-term forecasting, its assumptions and limitations should be carefully considered when interpreting the results.

Code and software output

Code to read data, fit the final model and produce forecasts

library(forecast)
Balances <- read.csv("USTradeBalances.csv", header = TRUE)
Y <- Balances$Services

fit <- arima(Y,
             order = c(1,1,1),
             seasonal = list(order = c(1,0,0), period = 12),
             include.mean = FALSE)

pred <- predict(fit, n.ahead = 12)

forecast_table <- data.frame(
  Month = c("2025-01","2025-02","2025-03","2025-04","2025-05","2025-06",
            "2025-07","2025-08","2025-09","2025-10","2025-11","2025-12"),
  Forecast = pred$pred,
  SE = pred$se,
  Lower95 = pred$pred - 1.96 * pred$se,
  Upper95 = pred$pred + 1.96 * pred$se
)

print(fit)
print(forecast_table)
